See the glucose of someone who shares it with you.

Ayni is a viewer for one person's continuous glucose — a partner, a parent, a child, someone in your care. It is nothing else: no loop, no pump, no insulin.

Readings are sealed on the sender's phone before they leave it, travel directly between phones, and only someone handed a key can open them. No account, no server holding your data, nobody in the middle who can read it.

It runs on a swarm, not a server: every phone and desktop running it quietly seeds a slice of everyone else's sealed data. The more people take part, the more resilient everyone's readings become — and no device can read a byte it holds for anyone else.

What Ayni is, and what it is not

Ayni does one job well and is honest about the rest. It shows glucose — it never touches a dose.

✓ It is

  • A viewer for one person's glucose, with every reading shown with its age.
  • End-to-end sealed: records are encrypted on the sender's phone, openable only with a key.
  • Revocable by key, not by a server's goodwill — stop wrapping the next key and sharing ends.
  • Serverless and accountless — readings travel directly between phones.
  • A standalone app (nz.diaswarm.ayni) that installs alongside AndroidAPS, never over it.

✕ It is not

  • An artificial pancreas. It contains no closed-loop code and does not talk to a pump.
  • Able to deliver insulin or change a dose. There is nothing in it to dose with.
  • A Nightscout server, or a thing that needs one.
  • A medical device. It's capable but early software — see the honest trade-offs.
  • A way to find or search for a person — there is no directory, deliberately.

Features

Sharing that you hold the key to

The whole design removes the server and puts access in one place: who holds a key.

Sealed in the middle

Records are sealed on the sender's phone before they are sent. Every holder in between stores ciphertext and can read none of it.

Revoke by key

Withdrawing access doesn't ask a server to stop serving. The departing reader is simply not wrapped for the next segment.

No account, no server

There is nobody to sign up with and no cloud holding your data. Readings travel phone to phone.

Fresh, and labelled with it

Readings normally arrive about 2–3 minutes old — plenty for a CGM — and each is shown with its age, so a stale one can't pose as current. (Android power-saving can delay a backgrounded phone; excuse it from battery optimisation for the freshest data.)

Carried by the community

Every Ayni phone and desktop peer holds a share of other people's sealed records, so your data stays reachable while your phone sleeps. More participants, more resilient — and none can read it.

Works off your network

On the same wifi it's direct. Anywhere else, an iroh relay introduces the two phones — and still cannot read a byte.

The swarm

A community holds the data up — not a company

There is no server behind Ayni. The swarm is simply everyone taking part, each holding a small, unreadable slice of everyone else's records, so no single phone has to be awake for the data to be there. Three kinds of device make it up:

Ayni phones

Every follower seeds a share of the pool for people it has never met — the app is named for that reciprocity. Following someone and carrying for the swarm are the same app.

Desktop peers

A diaswarm-peer on a machine that never sleeps turns "probably reachable" into "reliably reachable". One in a friend group lifts everyone in it.

The sender

The phone sharing its own loop — the diaswarm fork of AndroidAPS — publishes its sealed records into the same swarm, and hands no one a readable copy.

The more people share and seed, the more copies of everything exist and the more resilient everyone's data becomes. Every byte any device holds for anyone else is unreadable ciphertext.

Get connected

Install, then pair with one scan

Install Ayni from F-Droid. On its own it does nothing until someone shares with you — that's not a bug. Pairing is a single scan, and it works in both directions at once.

1

Install Ayni

From F-Droid. It installs alongside any other app and asks for nothing to open.

2

Show your code

Ayni shows a QR invite. Showing it opens a short window in which the sender's phone will accept being paired back — outside that moment, an uninvited offer is refused.

3

They scan it once

The person sharing scans your code. Their phone grants you access and hands back its own address in the same movement. The trend line appears.

One scan, not two. The invite's subject field is exactly the key someone would grant, so a single code works in both directions. An invite is not a secret and not a grant — anyone holding it can download your ciphertext and open none of it. A camera isn't always in the room, so an invite can also be pasted as text.

How it works

One reading, from the phone that recorded it to the phone that draws it

The padlock is on for the whole of the middle. The pool holds the ciphertext and serves it to anyone; only a granted reader's private key — which never leaves their device — can open it.

Desktop apps

A machine that doesn't sleep: diaswarm-peer

Every other holder is a phone, and the promise that your data stays reachable while your phone is asleep rests on someone having the bytes. One mains-powered desktop peer makes that structural rather than a matter of luck. It can read none of what it carries.

Match the version to your phone. The desktop peer and the Ayni app speak one wire protocol, and a mismatched build simply won't carry for a newer phone. Download the peer release that matches your Ayni version — current line is peer-v0.1.7, compatible with Ayni 0.1.7 — from the releases page. There's a native build for each platform, so running one is downloading a file, not installing a toolchain.

Run it with no arguments and it carries a share of the pool. It can also follow a subject you've been granted and turn that into something a clinic can use:

diaswarm-peer                                 # carry a share of the pool, read nothing
diaswarm-peer identity                        # the string a subject grants
diaswarm-peer read      --subject <id> --days 1
diaswarm-peer export    --subject <id> --out day.csv
diaswarm-peer summary   --subject <id> --days 90 --patient <id> --report agp.html
diaswarm-peer nightscout --subject <id> --out ./ns

summary is the clinician's view: an HL7 CGM FHIR document — time in range, mean glucose, GMI, variability, days of wear — that validates against HL7's own validator with zero errors. Nothing here ever dials a server; these are files you choose to hand over.

Note on Linux: run loginctl enable-linger "$USER" after enabling the service, or systemd stops it when you log out — so an "always-on" carrier runs only while you're logged in, which on a server is rarely.

Sending your own data

To share your own loop, you run the diaswarm fork of AndroidAPS

Ayni only receives. To be a sender — to share the glucose your own insulin-pump loop is already writing — the diaswarm module has to run inside AndroidAPS, and that means building AndroidAPS yourself.

Building AndroidAPS from source is normal — that part isn't new. The one thing that is specific to diaswarm: you must build from the diaswarm fork of AndroidAPS (the diaswarm-addon branch, which wires the module in), not upstream AndroidAPS. Upstream has no diaswarm module and cannot send. There is deliberately no pre-built loop APK distributed anywhere — running a loop is running a medical device you assembled, from source you have read.

The module ships disabled in the fork and is structurally incapable of dosing: it holds no pump reference and has no path into the loop. It only drains your own sealed records outward, and only once you've chosen to share. Build instructions and the full safety rationale are in the project repository.

Honest about the trade

What to know before you rely on it

Someone may make a decision at 3 a.m. based on what this shows, so the trade-offs are stated plainly rather than buried.

Built on proven foundations

The hard parts aren't home-made. Transport, membership and gossip are p2panda-net over iroh, and the group encryption that manages keys is p2panda-encryption — mainline, open-source libraries, not cryptography invented here. diaswarm adds a thin custody layer on top of them with standard, well-known primitives (X25519, ChaCha20-Poly1305). That layer hasn't yet had a formal third-party audit, so treat the whole as capable, actively developed software rather than something battle-hardened.

The honest costs

  • A relay sees metadata — never your readings, but which devices talk and when. The default is third-party; you can run your own.
  • Nothing can be un-published. Revocation stops future readings; it can't recall what was already shared.
  • Being in the swarm is visible, even though everything you hold for others is unreadable ciphertext.
  • Early days: no iOS yet, and storage grows over time — there's no pruning.

The full technical assessment lives in the repository's feasibility notes and decision log.
