a new shape for diabetes data

The data commons that can't be bought, captured, or switched off

Every diabetes data platform so far has had a middle — a server, a company, a foundation, a grant — and the data has only ever been as safe, as private, and as permanent as that middle. diaswarm removes the middle entirely. Your loop's data lives as sealed ciphertext spread across the community's own devices. Who can read it is decided by who holds a key. There is no host to trust, no operator to capture, and nothing anyone can switch off.

It already runs — on a live closed loop, phone to phone, across networks, with nobody in the middle. This is what comes after the server.

Honest scope, because this page is for people who will check: the custody and transport layers run today on Android/AAPS and the Ayni follower. An iOS follower does not exist yet (D12), and the research-export gateway is designed but not built — every claim below carries a tag for what actually ships. The cryptography is standard primitives in a composition that has had no third-party review (SECURITY.md).

When the host dies, the commons dies with it

For years, people have been able to donate their loop data to research through hosted data commons — you upload, an institution holds it, studies draw on it. Those pathways keep disappearing: a grant ends, a project winds down, priorities shift, and the commons goes dark with its host. That isn't bad luck — it's the design.

diaswarm is built so that can't happen. The commons isn't a place you upload to — it's a set of keys. The data lives, encrypted, on the devices of the people in it. No server to fund, no company to acquire, nothing to defund. A research project is just someone you hand a key to, and stop handing keys to when you're done.

The community already generates some of the richest health data in the world. It should outlive whoever happens to be hosting it this year.

One gesture. Every scale.

Sharing your glucose with your mum and feeding a global research cohort are the same act — seal data, hand a key to whoever you chose. Watch it scale.

Everything the Diabetes Data Rights Charter is asking for, running on a phone

People with diabetes spent seven roundtables across 16 countries writing down what control of their data should mean — the Diabetes Data Rights Charter. They described a system that doesn't exist in the commercial world. diaswarm is a working demonstration that it can be built: running code, not a policy asking a manufacturer to behave.

Consent & control that's real

Per person, by key, and withdrawable without asking a server's permission. Revoking one follower doesn't revoke everyone.

Privacy by design, in the strongest form

The people holding your data cannot read it — structurally, not as a policy they could quietly change.

Access with no vendor in the chain

Sensor to open source to your chosen reader. Nobody's cloud in the middle.

the honest part — because it's the project's style

The project keeps a public, unflinching self-assessment against all eight principles, including where it still falls short and what would close each gap. That document is itself the kind of transparency the Charter is asking industry to adopt. → docs/rights.md

You can't buy the middle, because there is no middle

Every company circling this space is selling the same thing: a place in the middle — the server you rent, the account they gate, the data they broker, the lock-in that keeps you. Centralised platforms can be monetised because there's a chokepoint to own.

What a custodian charges for

  • The host and the database the data lives in
  • The account and directory that gate who's who
  • The API that reads and writes it
  • Brokering the aggregate data onward to third parties
  • The friction of migrating off — lock-in

Each is a chokepoint someone owns.

What diaswarm leaves

  • No server to meter — data is ciphertext on participants' devices
  • No custodian holding the data to broker
  • No directory — you can't even search for a person, by design
  • Access is key custody; withdrawing asks no server's permission
  • AGPL, no APK sold, no account to lock in

No chokepoint to charge rent on.

The same thing that means nobody can sell it means nobody can capture it, subpoena it out of an operator, or pull its funding — because there's no operator and no funding to pull. It's AGPL, it's the community's, and it stays that way. The honest cost, said out loud: there's no company behind it either. What keeps it alive is the people who build and run it.

The questions change

Most worries about a system like this come from a lifetime of thinking in servers. Take the server away and the questions don't get harder — they change.

the old questionWhat happens when the server goes down — or when their phone is off?
the shiftThere's no server to go down, and no single phone the answer depends on. Many devices hold your data at once; the more people, the more available it gets, and it repairs itself as people come and go. Availability stops being "is the one machine up?" and becomes a property of the crowd.
the old questionHow do I edit the bad row in the database?
the shiftThere's no row to edit — the history is a signed, append-only ledger, which is why nobody can tamper with yours either. You correct forward: the right value is published and the trend reflects it, in the open, the way a ledger posts a correction rather than silently rewriting the past.
the old questionCan I make the server delete it?
the shift"Delete" on a server was always a polite fiction — backups, replicas, caches, whoever already copied it. Here the guarantee is honest and stronger where it counts: you stop handing out keys, and from that moment nothing new is readable to them, ever. What's already out is ciphertext nobody without a key can open. We'll never claim data was erased from the world, because no system can truthfully promise that.

None of these are things diaswarm fails to do. They're things the server made you expect in a shape that was never quite true. Same guarantees, told honestly — and, in the ways that actually protect you, stronger.

A role for Nightscout: seed the swarm, hold nothing

Keeping the network available isn't only on the users being followed. Anyone who wants to support it can strengthen it — at very low cost and effort. A single always-on device that carries a share of the swarm and serves it onward makes everyone's data more reachable, whether or not you follow a single person yourself.

Availability gets stronger the more always-on devices sit in the pool. A handful of seed boxes — funded by the Foundation and scattered around the world — would make it structural rather than a matter of how many followers happen to be awake. Each carries a share of the swarm's sealed data and can read none of it. A seed box breach is not a data breach.

Runs on anything

A small Rust binary. An old phone, a Raspberry Pi, a spare NAS, a retired laptop — mains power and a network connection is the whole spec. No battery, no Doze, so it's simply always there.

Costs next to nothing

A pool share is tens of megabytes a year and a trickle of bandwidth. Run one on hardware you were about to throw out, for roughly the electricity of a nightlight.

Holds nothing it can read

Every byte on a seed is ciphertext for people it was never granted. The Foundation strengthens the commons without ever becoming a custodian, a data controller, or a breach target.

Nothing central

Seeds don't coordinate and none is privileged — they just add always-on shoulders under the pool. Lose one and the arithmetic heals.

the honest sweetener

The pool has so far only run on a handful of phones. A dozen funded seed boxes wouldn't just harden availability — they'd be the first real test of the swarm at scale, the one thing the project most needs and can't fake with three devices. Funding the seeds is funding the proof.

It's the cleanest role there is for an organisation that cares about this community's data: make it more available to everyone, while holding none of it, reading none of it, and being liable for none of it.

Latency and availability

Two questions people who run Nightscout ask first: how old is the number when a follower sees it, and what happens when the phone that has the data is unreachable. Here is what the code does, what has been measured, and what hasn't.

The plugin seals and publishes every 60 s, or sooner once 32 KB of backfill is waiting (SHADOW_SEAL_INTERVAL_MS).
Holders
Pushed to over gossip as each entry is appended. There is no polling interval on this hop.
Reader
Polls every 120 s while awake. Measured at 105 s and 123 s between unattended refreshes.
Measured
2026-09-11: subject on mobile data behind CGNAT, reader on home wifi. Each refresh showed the newest reading as 0m old, and the screen read 9.4 mmol/L, 63 seconds old.

The reader isn't pushed to, by design. The subject doesn't know who follows them: the grant log names nobody, and a fetch doesn't say who is fetching. So the reader has to ask (D17).

In a pocket, Android sets the interval. Doze batches background work, so a reader phone that isn't excused from battery optimisation refreshes much less often. A held-open subscription would help while the app is open, but Doze closes sockets in the background either way. That's why every reading is drawn with its age, and counts as stale at 15 minutes.

When something is unreachable

What a reader gets in each case. The tags say how far each claim has been tested.

shown on phones tested off-device known gap

The subject's phone is off or out of signal

Readers fetch from the three holders, which verify and serve identical ciphertext. Nothing new arrives until the phone is back.

shown on phones with the reference vault: a stranger peer served a 73-day, 33,800-record history while the subject's phone was off.
tested off-device with the shipped vault: two peers in one process. Not yet repeated on phones (D28).

A holder leaves the pool

Every peer recomputes the pool size, bucket depth and top three holders on its next pass. Survivors pick up the gap. There's no repair process and nothing to elect.

tested off-device Disjoint shares need four or more devices. The pool has only ever been three phones.

Every holder is a phone, and they're all asleep

Doze, and Android killing the foreground service after about six hours, can leave no holder serving. A desktop peer is mains-powered and has neither problem, so one in the pool removes this case.

known gap on phones alone. The desktop peer (diaswarm-peer) is released for this.

The pool is smaller than three

With fewer than three peers, every peer holds every bucket, so there are as many copies as there are peers.

tested off-device depth_for(2) is 1, so both peers hold both buckets on every run.

The relay is unreachable

Phones on the same network still find each other over mDNS. Across networks, phones behind NAT can't find each other without a relay. It's the single third party in the design. The relay URL travels in the invite, so you can run your own.

shown on phones through n0's relay across CGNAT; LAN-only over mDNS.

The reader's phone has been quiet for hours

Ayni shows every reading's age. It tells "held but can't open" apart from "nothing sent" and reports which peers it couldn't reach. Nothing yet warns that the silence has gone on too long.

known gap No alert for long silence. Stale readings never raise glucose alerts.
